Europe dossier · Country file: Czechia

Czechia

Building under asset freeze. Agreement can be terminated. Russian House still open.

Czech authorities have established the Prague centre’s connection to the sanctioned Russian state agency Rossotrudnichestvo and placed the building under an asset freeze as an economic resource. The House itself, however, remains open. The intergovernmental agreement, in force since 1971 and terminable on six months’ notice, also remains in place.

● Active official site ● Financially or practically restricted
Core finding Czechia has neither closed the Russian House nor terminated its legal basis. Instead, it has treated the property as a sanctions matter. The building may not be sold, rented out, mortgaged or used to obtain funds, goods or services. This has not, however, halted the centre’s operations: events, library services, online activities and paid language courses continue. The agreement explains why the House can still operate; it does not prevent Czechia from ending that arrangement.

Timeline
  1. 12 May
    1971

    Intergovernmental agreement enters into force immediately

    The governments of the Czechoslovak Socialist Republic and the Soviet Union sign the agreement on the establishment and activities of the House in Prague. It enters into force the same day. Decree No. 81/1971

  2. 1971

    Opened in the shadow of the Prague Spring

    The House initially opens on Rytířská Street. Gustáv Husák and Leonid Brezhnev attend the ceremony. The institution is presented as a symbol of Soviet-Czechoslovak “friendship” after the crushing of the Prague Spring. Česká televize

  3. After
    1991

    Continued as a Russian cultural centre

    After the dissolution of the Soviet Union, the centre continues under Russian sponsorship. The Czech Ministry of Foreign Affairs still lists the 1971 agreement as a treaty in force with Russia. Czech Ministry of Foreign Affairs

  4. 21 July
    2022

    EU sanctions Rossotrudnichestvo

    The EU adds the operating agency to its sanctions list. The official grounds cite propaganda and public-diplomacy projects, Kremlin narratives and activities in occupied Ukrainian territories, among other matters. EU Decision 2022/1272

  5. 2022–
    2023

    Propaganda under the label of culture

    Investigative reporting documents Czech translations of Russian propaganda brochures. They blame Ukrainian forces and alleged “Nazi units” for civilian deaths in Mariupol, while presenting the annexation of Crimea as “reunification”. investigace.cz, 3 May 2023

  6. June
    2023

    Authorities investigate — the House remains

    The Financial Analytical Office examines possible links to the EU sanctions. At that time, the centre operates only on a limited basis and, according to Czech Television, opens mainly for individual events. No state closure order has been documented. ČT24, 5 June 2023

  7. 14 Nov.
    2024

    The property is placed under an asset freeze

    The Financial Analytical Office classifies the Na Zátorce building as an economic resource subject to the sanctions and imposes an asset freeze. The Russian Federation owns the property; the centre is both registered and physically based there. Ministry of Finance decision, pp. 1–4

  8. 27 Feb.
    2025

    Ministry of Finance confirms the freeze

    The ministry rejects the Russian appeal. In particular, Rossotrudnichestvo, the centre and the Russian Federation may not sell, rent out, mortgage or otherwise use the building to obtain funds, goods or services. The decision also states that the building is not part of the diplomatic mission and enjoys no diplomatic immunity. Ministry of Finance, 27 February 2025

  9. May
    2025

    Czech students visit the House

    A secondary-school group attends an event about Alexander Pushkin. According to the school, the students are also offered information about studying in Russia. The relevant regional governor calls the excursion “absolute madness” in view of Russia’s war of aggression. ČT24, 4 June 2025

  10. 26 March
    2026

    Incendiary attack

    Unknown perpetrators throw incendiary devices at the building. Czech authorities condemn the violent attack and police open an investigation. The incident does not alter the legal and political assessment of the centre. ČT24, 27 March 2026

  11. July
    2026

    Operations remain active

    The House continues to advertise events, library services, its own politically framed historical content and paid language courses. The asset freeze has not brought its operations to an end. Current full programme · Course timetable and fees 2025/26

Legal basis

The legal basis is the intergovernmental agreement of 12 May 1971, published as Decree No. 81/1971. The Czech Ministry of Foreign Affairs continues to list it among the treaties in force with Russia. Treaty register · Full text

Political information was part of the treaty mandate

The House was intended to consolidate the “eternal and indestructible friendship”, inform the public about the achievements of Soviet communism and present Soviet foreign policy.

Propaganda material was expressly envisaged

Article 6 permits lectures, political information work, press conferences and the distribution of Soviet news, publications and information materials.

Subject to the law of the host country

Under Article 3, the House is an independent legal entity. Its activities are governed by the law of the host country, and the director is responsible for compliance.

No diplomatic immunity

The agreement does not grant the House diplomatic immunity. The Ministry of Finance has also confirmed that the present building is not part of the Russian diplomatic mission.

„Tato dohoda se uzavírá na neomezenou dobu a může být písemně vypovězena každou ze smluvních stran na základě šestiměsíční výpovědní lhůty.“
English translation: “This agreement is concluded for an indefinite period and may be terminated in writing by either contracting party subject to six months’ notice.”
Article 11 of the agreement
Crucially: Czechia is not permanently bound by the agreement. Under its wording, the government could terminate it unilaterally; it would cease to apply six months later.
Measures since 2022

The authorities examined the sanctions nexus and placed the building under an asset freeze as an economic resource. The official decision attributes the centre entirely to Rossotrudnichestvo: the agency exercises 100 per cent control within the meaning of sanctions law. The building itself, by contrast, is owned by the Russian Federation. Ministry of Finance: appeal decision

Documented

  • Investigation of possible sanctions violations
  • Asset freeze on the building and property
  • Ban on sale, rental, mortgaging and use of the property to obtain funds, goods or services
  • Finding of control by Rossotrudnichestvo
  • No diplomatic immunity for the building
  • Political and public calls for closure

Not documented

  • Formal closure of the Russian House
  • General ban on events or operations
  • Termination or suspension of the 1971 agreement
  • Inclusion of the Prague House itself on a Czech sanctions list
  • Final court ruling on the challenge to the building freeze

The asset freeze is therefore not a closure. It restricts dealings with and economic exploitation of the property, but it has halted neither ongoing operations nor the advertising of fee-charging courses: for the second semester of 2025/26, the website lists in-person and online courses priced at up to CZK 7,200. How those revenues are handled under sanctions law — and whether a licence or exemption applies — remains unclear. At the end of 2025, proceedings against the decision were pending before the Prague Municipal Court; by the editorial cut-off date, we could not identify a later final judgment. Russian House Prague: course timetable and fees 2025/26 · iROZHLAS, 12 November 2025

Foreign Ministry explanation in the original

No formal government decision to keep the House open is known. In June 2023, however, Czech Foreign Ministry spokesman Daniel Drake stated:

„Ruské kulturní centrum má statut objektu pro státní neobchodní účely, je vázané mezinárodní dohodou, která je stále platná. Diplomatický servis tuto dohodu respektuje a neúčtuje si žádné nájemné.“
English translation: “The Russian Cultural Centre has the status of a property used for state, non-commercial purposes and is governed by an international agreement that remains in force. The diplomatic service respects this agreement and charges no rent.”
ČT24, 5 June 2023

What this statement says — and what it does not

The ministry explained why the existing agreement is being respected administratively. It did not say that Czechia was unable to terminate it. Article 11 expressly permits unilateral termination. The agreement is therefore a continuing legal basis, but not a legal obstacle to ending it.

Assessment

Czechia is a particularly revealing case. The country recognises the propagandistic nature of the activities, examines compliance with sanctions and freezes the building — but does not terminate the underlying agreement.

There is a further distinction: political influence work here is not merely a later diversion from a neutral cultural mission. The 1971 agreement expressly envisaged the presentation of Soviet foreign policy and the distribution of political information materials. What is now criticised as a fusion of culture and propaganda formed part of the institution’s mandate from the outset.

Romania

The government stated that the centre had abandoned its cultural mission and become an instrument of propaganda, disinformation and the justification of Russian war crimes. Its operations were suspended.

Czechia

The historical agreement itself envisages political information and propaganda work. It nevertheless remains in force; so far, the state has limited itself to an asset freeze on the property without prohibiting the centre’s operations.

Political responsibility

Under Petr Fiala, Czechia was among Ukraine’s most determined supporters. His government organised military and humanitarian aid and drove the international ammunition initiative. Yet it was this same government that did not terminate the 1971 agreement. Czech Government, 5 May 2025

Under Andrej Babiš, a more decisive step appears even less likely. His government permits the ammunition initiative to continue only as long as other states finance it and has ruled out using Czech taxpayers’ money or sending Czech troops to Ukraine. There is, however, no evidence that Babiš personally intervened on behalf of the Russian House. Czech Government, 6 January 2026

President Petr Pavel continues to support a pro-Ukrainian course. He cannot, however, terminate the agreement on his own against the government: presidential foreign-policy decisions under Article 63 generally require countersignature by the prime minister or an authorised member of the government. We found no public initiative by Pavel specifically calling for termination of the agreement or closure of the Russian House. Pavel on Ukraine, 24 September 2025 · Constitution, Article 63

Editorial assessment

Czechia is not legally bound but politically blocked. Fiala could have terminated the agreement, but did not. Babiš is unlikely to want to. Pavel could press the issue publicly, but cannot terminate the agreement himself — and appears not to have chosen this particular fight.

The legal powers and political decisions are documented. The assessment of motives is an inference from the documented course of action; no clear public government answer has been given as to why the agreement was not terminated.
