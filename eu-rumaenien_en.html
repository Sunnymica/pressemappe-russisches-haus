Romania country file

Opened as a cultural centre — then branded a propaganda instrument

Agreed in 2013, opened in 2015 and suspended in 2023: Romania said the Russian Centre for Science and Culture in Bucharest had become an instrument of propaganda, disinformation and justification of Russian war crimes. The case also shows why “closed” and “fully dismantled in law” are not necessarily the same thing.

Operations suspended Residual legal status unclear

At a glance

Centre Russian Centre for Science and Culture in Bucharest
Operator Rossotrudnichestvo; the centre served as the agency’s representation in Romania
Address 50 Bulevardul Lascăr Catargiu, Bucharest
Agreement Signed on 9 July 2013
Approval Government Decision No. 834 of 25 October 2013; published on 5 November 2013
Opening 15 May 2015
Measure Suspension of activities; administrative implementation due by 20 August 2023 at the latest
Status in July 2026 Public operations remain suspended. Bucharest no longer appears as a location on Rossotrudnichestvo’s current map. The Romanian Foreign Ministry’s diplomatic directory of 29 June 2026 nevertheless still lists the centre, its address and contact details.
Official grounds Disinformation, distortion of history, hybrid influence activity and justification of Russian war crimes

The crucial distinction

Romania suspended the centre’s activities. No publicly accessible measure has yet been found that formally terminates the bilateral agreement or conclusively abolishes the centre’s institutional status. Its continued appearance in the 2026 diplomatic directory does not prove that operations resumed. It does show, however, that operational shutdown and formal legal closure can be two separate processes.

Legal basis

The legal basis was the bilateral government agreement signed on 9 July 2013. It created two reciprocal institutions: the Romanian Cultural Institute in Moscow and the Russian Centre for Science and Culture in Bucharest. Romania approved the agreement through Government Decision No. 834 of 25 October 2013.

https://legislatie.just.ro/Public/FormaPrintabila/00000G2E1OZFIO9Q15S374NS7ARU4M74

The centre was therefore not an ordinary private cultural initiative. It formed part of a structure formally agreed by the two governments. Its connection to Rossotrudnichestvo is also supported by the attendance of the agency’s then director at the opening and by Russia’s later official response.

https://www.bursa.ro/centrul-rus-de-536tiin539a-537i-cultura-s-a-deschis-in-capitala-04639620
https://romania.mid.ru/ro/press-centre/arkhiv_novostey/declara_ia_purt_torului_de_cuv_nt_al_mae_maria_zaharova_cu_privire_la_activit_ile_centrului_rus_de_t/

No separate public legal act has yet been identified that formally terminates the agreement. What is documented is the suspension of the centre’s activities. Whether Romania relied on a treaty clause, a unilateral suspension of application or another administrative mechanism remains to be established.

Measures since 2022

Romania allowed the centre to continue operating for roughly a year after the full-scale invasion began. Only on 21 February 2023 did the government decide to suspend its activities and summon the Russian ambassador.

https://mae.ro/node/61159

The official justification focused squarely on the institution’s own conduct: repeated and deliberate distortion of reality and historical truth, propaganda in support of the war of aggression, disinformation by omission and intentional manipulation, and attempts to justify Russian war crimes. The Foreign Ministry placed these practices within the broader context of Russian hybrid attacks.

The government’s reasoning in the original

Romanian Foreign Ministry · 21 February 2023
„Centrul s-a îndepărtat iremediabil de obiectivele firești de consolidare a legăturilor culturale și s-a transformat, în mod regretabil, într-un instrument de propagandă, dezinformare și disculpare a crimelor de război ale Federației Ruse din Ucraina.“
English translation:
“The centre has irreversibly departed from the natural objective of strengthening cultural ties and has regrettably turned into an instrument of propaganda, disinformation and exculpation of the Russian Federation’s war crimes in Ukraine.”
https://mae.ro/node/61159

The ministry also accused the centre of repeatedly and deliberately promoting distorted versions of reality and historical truth in Romanian public debate. It described Russia’s conduct as hybrid aggression and disinformation through omission and deliberate manipulation of reality.

https://mae.ro/node/61159

Assessment

From political accommodation to public repudiation

The trajectory is striking. Romania agreed to the centre in 2013, opened it in 2015 despite Russia’s annexation of Crimea, and in 2023 described it as an instrument of propaganda and disinformation. The decisive factor appears to have been not merely the institution’s existence, but the escalation of its own conduct after the full-scale invasion.

For the German comparison, one point matters in particular: the bilateral agreement did not prevent state action. Romania did not necessarily dismantle the entire treaty framework at once, but it suspended the centre’s activities and imposed an administrative deadline. A treaty was therefore not treated as a licence for indefinite operation.

The Romanian case also warns against overly simple labels. Politically, the centre was shut down. Yet its continued appearance in the 2026 diplomatic directory shows that a public suspension does not automatically erase every legal, protocol-related or organisational trace.
