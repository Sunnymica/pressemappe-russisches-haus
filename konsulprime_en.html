Newly documented

A Dubai-based service provider inside the Russian House Berlin

Since February 2026, the Russian House Berlin at Friedrichstraße 176–179 has hosted a partner office for Russian consular documents. According to Konsul Prime, a German company based in Bonn acts as the local partner. The commercial use raises questions under EU sanctions law and about the building’s immunity from enforcement.

The research into this structure was first published by researcher Vic (@vic_010100) on 6 August 2026. The account below reproduces the documented findings from that research and adds an assessment of the sanctions and immunity questions.

What is operating on Friedrichstraße

Konsul Prime is a service provider that assists Russian citizens abroad with Russian documents, including foreign passports, powers of attorney, notarised certifications and translations. The legal entity is Konsul Prime DMCC, based in Dubai and incorporated under the law of the United Arab Emirates.

A partner office opened inside the Russian House in February 2026. According to Konsul Prime’s own information, the local premises are not operated by Konsul Prime itself but by Visa Support & Business Solutions GmbH, based in Bonn, which acts as the location partner. Konsul Prime advertises the Berlin address as a point of contact for customers in Germany, open Monday to Friday from 9:00 to 18:00.

The company’s service hotline is a Moscow landline number; according to the provider, calls are free for callers in Moscow. According to registry data documented by @vic_010100, a Russian limited liability company under the name “Консул Прайм” was also registered in Moscow on 4 June 2026. That registry entry has not yet been independently verified for this version.

Its proximity to Russian consular structures

Konsul Prime does not present itself simply as a private service provider. On the website of the Embassy of the Russian Federation in Germany, the company is described as an authorised representative of Russian consulates abroad and as an information and advisory centre. According to that page, it is authorised to support Russian missions in certain consular procedures, in particular by checking documents in advance and advising applicants.

In its own materials, Konsul Prime describes itself as the sole official partner of Russian consulates abroad and states that it is accredited by the Russian Foreign Ministry to provide these services.

According to a notice published by Rossotrudnichestvo, the company has also been involved since 1 July 2026 in a state-coordinated pilot project initially covering twelve countries. The project is intended to give Russian citizens abroad access to digital government services, including qualified electronic signatures that can be used to sign legally binding documents remotely. According to the notice, the programme is coordinated by the Russian Foreign Ministry together with Rossotrudnichestvo.

How to read the sources

The claims concerning accreditation and the company’s role as a consular partner come from Konsul Prime’s own materials and websites of Russian state bodies. They must be cited as such and are not independently established facts. The pages identified with archive links in the source list were preserved on 7 August 2026; no archive copy of the Rossotrudnichestvo notice is currently available.

A second layer of fees

Fees for consular acts are set by the Russian state. The current fee schedule was approved by Order No. 178 of the Embassy of the Russian Federation in Germany on 27 July 2026 and entered into force on 1 August 2026. For each consular act, it lists the total amount and separates the consular fee itself from the amount charged to reimburse actual costs.

In addition to these state-set fees, Konsul Prime charges its own service fee. The company explains in its knowledge base that applicants pay this fee for the service and that it is set at a level sufficient to cover the company’s operating, administrative and business costs abroad.

What is established is this: in addition to state-set consular charges, Konsul Prime levies private service fees and says that these finance its operating and administrative costs abroad. This alone does not show whether, or to what extent, the Berlin location is financed from those fees.

Why this raises sanctions questions

Rossotrudnichestvo, the operator of the Russian House, has been on the European Union sanctions list since 21 July 2022. Article 2 of Regulation (EU) No 269/2014 freezes the funds and economic resources of listed entities and, as a general rule, prohibits funds or economic resources from being made available to them directly or indirectly. Exceptions and authorisations are possible under specified conditions.

The sanctions regime already has practical consequences for the Russian House. Transfers from frozen funds are controlled, and expenditure must be shown to fall within authorised preservation and maintenance purposes. In 2025, the Federal Foreign Office told tagesschau.de that rent payments would breach the prohibition if they benefited the listed person or entity.

This is why the partner office matters. It is not publicly known whether Visa Support & Business Solutions GmbH pays for the rooms, who would receive any such payment, or whether any required sanctions authorisation exists. Without those facts, no sanctions violation can be established. They are nevertheless central to assessing whether the commercial use is compatible with the prohibition on making funds or economic resources available.

Free use of the rooms would not automatically constitute a sanctions violation either. It would, however, raise the question of the contractual basis on which a private company uses rooms in a property controlled by Rossotrudnichestvo for commercial purposes and whether the listed operator derives any indirect economic benefit from that arrangement.

Visa Support & Business Solutions GmbH is a German company. If it uses the rooms for payment, the corresponding contractual and payment relationship would in principle form part of its business records. No such contract is publicly available. Konsul Prime DMCC states in its own legal notice that it has no German or EU VAT identification number and instead lists a United Arab Emirates tax registration number.

Why this matters for immunity from enforcement

The building’s immunity from enforcement has already been considered by German courts. In 2009, the Berlin Regional Court upheld the cancellation of compulsory administration and a forced sale because there were sufficient indications that the Russian House was being used for sovereign diplomatic purposes. According to the reasoning reported at the time, enforcement against the property of a foreign state would be permissible only where the asset in question was clearly not being used for sovereign purposes. The Russian Embassy had referred, among other things, to apartments for diplomats inside the building.

Today, in addition to cultural operations, the Russian House contains a bookshop, cinema, event spaces, a Russian Post acceptance point, a translation agency and residential accommodation. Official statistics do not identify how many people live in the building itself, for data-protection reasons. As of 30 June 2023, 115 principal residences were registered in the relevant city block; tagesschau.de reported that it could identify no residential use in the other buildings within that block.

A commercial partner office run by a private service provider is therefore relevant to the legal assessment: a third party is using rooms in the building to provide paid services. This does not automatically mean that the immunity from enforcement of the entire building disappears. Whether and to what extent such commercial use changes the international-law assessment would have to be determined by a court in any future dispute.

Open questions

The findings raise three questions for the Federal Foreign Office and the competent authorities. First: are they aware that a commercial service office is operating in a property run by Rossotrudnichestvo? Second: are payments made for the use of the rooms, who receives them, and is there a sanctions authorisation if one is required? Third: what significance does commercial use by a third party have for the previous international-law classification of the building as state property used, at least in part, for sovereign purposes?
