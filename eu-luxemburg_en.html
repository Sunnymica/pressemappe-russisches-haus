Luxembourg country file

Status agreement documented, full text not findable

Luxembourg has an official centre underpinned by two bilateral legal acts. On 16 March 2010 the governments concluded a special agreement on status and operation which, according to the foreign ministry’s annual report, entered into force immediately. That this agreement exists is documented – what it contains is not: the full authentic text was not found.

The existence of an agreement is not knowledge of its content

The Luxembourg case shows an often overlooked evidentiary gap: that a status agreement was concluded and entered into force immediately is documented by the official annual report. What legal personality, what tax and customs rules, what notice period and what staff privileges it contains is unknown. The existence of a treaty does not confer knowledge of its legal effects. Reading rule: “Open” means the statement is not documented in the public sources examined. It is not proof of the contrary. EU listing, national enforcement, political non-cooperation, treaty termination and actual closure of operations are assessed separately.

Legal basis

Two legal acts underpin the centre. The general agreement of 28 June 1993 was approved by the act of 9 April 1996 and published in full in Mémorial A No 28. Article 30 contains an express clause on duration and termination – the treaty is therefore terminable.

https://legilux.public.lu

The special agreement of 16 March 2010 on the status and operation of the centre is documented by the foreign ministry’s annual report for 2010, including immediate entry into force. Where the full authentic text is published or deposited could not be established.

https://download.data.public.lu

The central legal questions therefore remain open: the centre’s legal personality, tax and customs rules, notice period and staff privileges. Whether either agreement was formally terminated or amended is likewise open – continued activity is only an indication, not proof that no unpublished note exists.

https://legilux.public.lu
https://download.data.public.lu

Measures since 2022

Luxembourg enforces the EU sanctions robustly: an interministerial committee was established in March 2022 and almost 2.5 billion euros in bank assets and holdings were reported as frozen. These figures are general and not attributed to the house.

https://gouvernement.lu

On 6 April 2022 Luxembourg expelled a Russian embassy diplomat. Here too no link to the house was published. No termination of the status agreement and no asset measure relating to the house were found.

https://lequotidien.lu

Assessment

Four levels of assessment kept apart by a small state

Luxembourg shows that a small host state does not automatically terminate a formal cultural treaty even while enforcing sanctions robustly and reducing diplomatic staff. Both happened side by side without replacing one another.

For Germany the precise treaty wording would be decisive. The Luxembourg case distinguishes four separate levels of assessment: general cultural treaty, centre-specific status agreement, financial sanctions law and actual operation of the house.

It also marks a methodological warning for this dossier as a whole: where a status treaty exists but is not publicly accessible, statements about immunities, taxes and notice periods simply cannot be made – not even in cautious terms.
