Malta country file

Self-closure after threats – not a state order

Malta has had an official Russian cultural centre in Valletta since 1990. It closed temporarily to the public from 3 March 2022 – after receiving threats and after police were called in. There is no evidence of a state closure order. Later events document its reopening and continuing on-site operations.

Self-closure is not state termination

The Maltese case is easily misclassified. The centre closed itself and temporarily in March 2022 after receiving threats and, according to media reports, “very violent messages”. The foreign minister met the director and appealed for social peace. A state suspension or termination is therefore precisely not evidenced. The exact date on which operations resumed was not found. Reading rule: “Open” means the statement is not documented in the public sources examined. It is not proof of the contrary. EU listing, national enforcement, political non-cooperation, treaty termination and actual closure of operations are assessed separately.

Legal basis

The Maltese legal trail begins earlier than often assumed – with the exchange of notes of March 1987. It is, however, largely documented through Russian government decisions approving drafts, not through signed final treaties.

https://www.lawmix.ru
https://pravo.gov.ru

Decision No 1490 of 14 December 1996 evidences approval of an agreement on the centre’s activity. When that agreement was signed and brought into force, and where the authentic text lies, is open.

https://pravo.gov.ru

As long as the authentic texts are missing, legal personality, diplomatic status, ownership, taxes and immunity remain unresolved. The 1994 draft describes the centre as a non-profit organisation under embassy direction – that too is the content of a draft and no evidence of law in force.

https://www.lawmix.ru

Measures since 2022

Malta implemented the EU sanctions and published guidance through the financial intelligence unit FIAU and the Sanctions Monitoring Board. No enforcement act relating to the house – freezing of accounts, a freezing order or an operating ban – was found.

https://fiaumalta.org
https://smb.gov.mt

Unlike most EU states, Malta expelled no Russian diplomats. The government cited the very small Maltese mission in Moscow and the risk of reciprocal incapacity.

https://tvmnews.mt

The temporary closure of the centre from 3 March 2022 was not the result of a state order but of threats against the institution. The foreign minister sought a meeting with the director in the same week.

https://timesofmalta.com
https://lovinmalta.com

Assessment

Two dividing lines Malta makes particularly clear

Malta shows two dividing lines. First: a temporary, security-driven self-closure is not a state suspension. Equating the two attributes to the Maltese state a measure it did not take.

Second: a centre can continue operating despite the EU listing of its operator where payment routes, derogations or embassy structures allow it. But no general sanctions exemption may be inferred from this without documentary evidence – the question of how ongoing operations are financed remains unanswered.

For Germany the full treaty, the actual account holders and official authorisations would be the key documents. In the Maltese case all three are publicly missing.
