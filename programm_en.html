The Programme of the Russian House

Behind an apparently harmless facade lies a highly calculated, multi-layered agenda. An analysis.

“They are remarkably clever in how they design their programme. At first glance, it appears completely innocuous.”

The Russian House Berlin offers concerts, language courses, children's film screenings, and art exhibitions. To the casual observer, this looks like genuine cultural exchange. Upon closer inspection, however, the curation reveals a spectrum ranging from overt state propaganda to subtle cultural self-projection – and that is precisely wherein its true effectiveness lies.

Not every film screened here is propaganda in the classical sense. Some are genuine works of art. Others are heartwarming children's movies or poetic art house cinema. Yet, together they build a cohesive narrative: Russia portrayed as a profound, soulful, and culturally rich nation – entirely detached from the realities of war, international sanctions, and the atrocities committed in Bucha.

Below are four illustrative examples from the current programme.

Overt

State-commissioned blockbusters, Kremlin awards, Roscosmos partnerships

Subtle

A focus on the great classical poets, tragic beauty, and the Russian soul

Indirect

Children's films, idyllic landscapes, and a seemingly apolitical atmosphere

1
Cinema · April 9, 2026
The Challenge
Director: Klim Shipenko · Russia 2023

Promoted as the first feature film shot on the International Space Station, this movie tells the story of a Russian surgeon who flies to the ISS to perform emergency surgery on a cosmonaut. The film is a high-profile co-production between Roscosmos and the Russian state broadcaster Channel One. The underlying message is unmistakable: Russia remains a pioneering global superpower, regardless of international sanctions or geopolitical isolation.

Roscosmos described the project as “a unique scientific and educational endeavor.” In reality, the production timeline was aggressively expedited to beat Hollywood and Tom Cruise to this cinematic milestone, mirroring the competitive spirit of the Cold War space race.

Verified Fact: Russian President Vladimir Putin personally awarded director Shipenko and lead actress Peresild a prestigious state prize at the Kremlin on June 12, 2023. The Russian House screened this film on April 9, 2026.
2
Cinema · 2025/2026 Season
Lermontov
Director: Boris Bakuradze · Russia

A poetic, atmospheric art house portrait of the 19th-century classical poet Mikhail Lermontov. Characterized by long takes and a lack of conventional Hollywood dramaturgy, the film focuses on mood, melancholy, and inner existential torment. This is not an overt, state-directed film, which is precisely what makes its placement so effective.

The propagandistic value lies not in what is actively projected, but in what is deliberately omitted: the ongoing war, civilian casualties, and international isolation. Instead, the audience is presented with Russia as a sanctuary of towering literary figures, profound emotion, and tragic beauty. This sophisticated cultural image resonates well with international audiences.

Contextual Assessment: No state commission or direct political message. However, it is an integral part of a cultural schedule designed to project normalcy and high culture while Russian missiles target Ukrainian cities.
3
Cinema · 2025/2026 Season
The Adventures of Little Baha
Director: Aleksandr Galibin · Russia

A heartwarming family movie centered on an eight-year-old boy named Baha living in a remote mountain village in Dagestan. The film features no villains, no obvious political agenda, and no ideological rhetoric. Instead, it offers rugged Caucasian landscapes, authentic characters, and a universal story about childhood and growing up.

Because the movie is entirely harmless on its own, it disarms suspicion. A children’s film acts as a disarming agent: it invites families into the center, builds goodwill, and projects an organic, humanizing image of Russian society. While the depicted reality is not false, the selection of such content within this specific venue is highly strategic.

Contextual Assessment: Not a propaganda film in the classical sense. However, when screened in this building at this particular time, it serves the clear purpose of normalizing the institution.
4
Exhibition · Since March 2025
“The Russian Soul of the White Rose”
On Alexander Schmorell · Under the Patronage of the Russian Embassy

Alexander Schmorell was a co-founder of the White Rose (Weiße Rose) anti-Nazi resistance group in Munich, executed by the National Socialists in 1943. Born in Russia, he moved to Germany at the age of four, and Russian remained his mother tongue throughout his life.

The Russian House exhibition focuses heavily on the “Russian soul” and Eastern Orthodox faith of this resistance icon, operating under the official patronage of the Russian Embassy. Crucially, this exhibition runs concurrently with the Kremlin’s broader narrative depicting its current military actions in Ukraine as a continuation of the anti-fascist struggle.

A historic victim of National Socialism is posthumously instrumentalized as a projection screen for contemporary imperial mythology. Rather than a mere interpretation, this aligns directly with the narrative logic of the exhibition in this specific context.

Verified Fact: The exhibition has been running at the Russian House Berlin since March 2025 under the official patronage of the Russian Embassy. Analysis by journalist Regina Laska, published on antwortseiten.org, June 19, 2025.

The Pattern

No single film screening or art exhibition proves political influence in isolation. The systematic pattern only emerges from the combination of events: mainstream state-backed cinema presented alongside independent art house projects, family-friendly children’s films, and historical memorial exhibitions. This curated blend ensures the programme appeals broadly, avoids immediate pushback, and projects the image of a sophisticated, peaceful cultural nation.

This occurs while military strikes on Ukrainian cities continue, political dissidents are silenced, and international sanctions remain largely circumvented.

The Russian House is not a crude propaganda machine; it is a highly sophisticated one, which is precisely why its influence is so potent.

A final significant observation: approximately 90 percent of the films screened at the venue are related to war and historical conflict, despite the director's persistent claims that the institution is entirely non-political. These films either reinforce a specific Russian historical perspective – framing the Second World War primarily as a Soviet triumph – or portray Russia as a historically resilient, enduring nation. Any critical perspective regarding the current war against Ukraine is entirely absent.

Regina Laska is an independent journalist and author. She writes for FOCUS+ and publishes investigative analyses on antwortseiten.org. This report is based on independent research and publicly accessible sources.
