A European comparison

Russian Houses across Europe: How other countries have responded

Germany continues to allow the Russian House in Berlin to operate. Elsewhere in Europe, governments have closed Russian cultural centres, suspended their activities or curtailed official representations. Yet the picture is more complicated than Rossotrudnichestvo’s official presentation suggests. Some countries never had a public-facing Russian House; in others, earlier structures quietly disappeared, or Russia continued its outreach through embassies, associations, schools and hubs in neighbouring countries. These country files examine which tools other governments have used — and what those choices imply for Germany’s handling of the Russian House in Berlin.

Romania Operations ended Residual legal status unclear Bucharest suspended the centre’s operations in 2023; some formal entries remained.

Bucharest hosted a formally established Russian Centre for Science and Culture under a bilateral agreement signed in 2013. On 21 February 2023, Romania’s Foreign Ministry informed the Russian ambassador that the centre’s activities were being suspended. Operations had to end by 20 August 2023.

The government explicitly cited propaganda, disinformation, distorted accounts of historical facts and attempts to justify Russian war crimes. Bucharest is absent from Rossotrudnichestvo’s current map, although continuing registry entries mean that the residual formal status has not been clarified in every detail.

Key finding: Romania required a formally established centre to cease operating. Its activities ended, while the full contractual and registry consequences remain a separate legal question.

Republic of Moldova · non-EU Operations ended Embassy structure remains active Moldova terminated the agreement in time; the Russian House closed on 4 July 2026.

The Russian House in Chișinău operated under a 1998 agreement on cultural centres. Article 17 provided for automatic five-year renewals unless either side gave written notice at least six months before the end of the current term. After further Russian drone incursions into Moldovan airspace, the government began the termination procedure in 2025; Russia was formally notified on 3 December 2025.

The agreement expired on 4 July 2026, and the official Russian House ceased operating the same day. Rossotrudnichestvo nevertheless retained a representative inside the Russian embassy, where education quotas and other so-called humanitarian programmes are expected to continue.

Key finding: Moldova actually used the termination clause built into the agreement. The official house closed, but some functions shifted into the Russian embassy.

Czechia Financially and operationally restricted Programmes still active The property became a sanctions case, while cultural, language and online services continued.

Czechia treated the building and its commercial use as a sanctions matter. Revenue and economic exploitation were heavily restricted. That did not amount to a political closure of the Russian House.

Events, language courses, the library and online services continued. The general cultural agreement also remained in force despite being terminable. The legal framework explains the present intermediate situation; it did not compel the government to preserve it.

Key finding: Prague demonstrates the difference between sanctions against an operator and its property, on the one hand, and the continued operation of the institution, on the other.

Bulgaria Official site active Tax proceedings pending Sofia acknowledged the potential sanctions implications in 2024; in 2026 a tax audit still had not begun.

The Russian Cultural and Information Centre in Sofia remains active. Bulgaria explicitly acknowledged the centre’s potential sanctions exposure in 2024 but did not implement corresponding measures. In 2026 the tax authority found taxable economic activity without the corresponding tax returns and tax payments.

A Sega investigation also documented numerous visits by municipal schools and kindergartens after 24 February 2022. The basement additionally houses the third-party-operated “Fotonika” fighter-aircraft simulator, with offers explicitly available from the age of six.

Key finding: Bulgaria is not a closure case. The problem was recognised politically and under tax law, but the official centre remained in operation.

Poland In-person operations suspended Digital structure remains active Poland did not close it: Rossotrudnichestvo suspended the Warsaw site’s in-person operations in 2026.

Warsaw hosted an official Russian centre. Poland recognised its propaganda and disinformation role but refrained from ordering a state closure, partly because of the Polish Institute in Moscow and the principle of reciprocity.

In February 2026, Rossotrudnichestvo itself suspended in-person operations and shifted responsibilities to Budapest. Digital channels remained active. No documented causal link has been found between a Polish government measure and the Russian withdrawal, and the underlying agreement was not terminated.

Key finding: Warsaw is not a Polish closure case. It was a state-tolerated site from which Russia later withdrew its own in-person operation.

Finland Financially and legally restricted Institution still active Accounts were frozen and the property placed under legal attachment, but the institution remained in place.

Finland enforced EU sanctions against Rossotrudnichestvo and froze accounts. The Russian House property was seized or placed under legal attachment in connection with Ukrainian compensation claims in the Naftogaz proceedings.

Enforcement was ordered but has not become final because Russia appealed. The institution nevertheless remained organisationally and digitally active. Finland also left the cultural agreement in force, even though it can be terminated with six months’ notice.

Key finding: Helsinki shows how far sanctions and enforcement proceedings can go without the state terminating the underlying agreement or politically ending the institution.

Denmark Regular in-person operations ended Intermittent use reported A staffing cap ended regular operations in 2023; since 2025 the Russian embassy has reported individual events.

Copenhagen hosted an official Russian Centre for Science and Culture. Denmark did not close it through a measure directed specifically at the institution. Instead, in September 2023 it capped the Russian mission as a whole at five diplomats and 20 administrative and technical staff. Regular operations then ceased.

The centre has been absent from Denmark’s diplomatic list since 2024. Russian embassy channels nevertheless began reporting individual events in May 2025 at a venue they continued to call the Russian House or RCSC. That demonstrates intermittent use, not the restoration of the former full-scale Rossotrudnichestvo operation.

Key finding: Denmark effectively shut down regular operations through a general diplomatic staffing measure. Because the terminable cultural agreement remained in force, the institution’s residual status has not been fully resolved.

Croatia No official house established Tax proceedings pending Former structure no longer traceable The planned centre was never implemented by treaty; a later substitute representation disappeared after 2022.

The general cultural agreement between Croatia and Russia did not establish a centre. A separate agreement on information and cultural centres was envisaged in 2013, but in 2015 it was explicitly recorded as not implemented.

Even so, a Rossotrudnichestvo representation can be documented by 2018 and the public brand “Russian House Zagreb” by 2021. After Croatia drastically reduced the Russian embassy in 2022, regular activity was no longer publicly traceable. Later sources referred to suspension, but no Croatian closure act has been found.

Key finding: Croatia is not a classic closure case. A centre that was never fully institutionalised was replaced by a later representation that subsequently disappeared from public view.

Netherlands Former representation disappeared Partner network active No current house, but a decentralised network spanning the embassy, associations, schools and Brussels.

There is no official Russian House in the Netherlands today. An official Rossotrudnichestvo country representation can nevertheless be documented in 2013 and 2014. It apparently had no dedicated public cultural building and had disappeared by 2017 at the latest, well before the full-scale invasion.

Its functions survived in decentralised form through the Russian embassy, the KCOPC/KSORS compatriot council, schools, cultural associations, honorary consulates, the University of Groningen and the Russian House in Brussels. Cooperation by local organisations with Rossotrudnichestvo does not by itself prove financing, ownership or command.

Key finding: The absence of a sign on the door does not mean the absence of a structure. The Dutch model is smaller, more decentralised and harder to see.

Belgium Official site active Legal status open Belgium expelled 21 Russian diplomats – the house in Brussels stayed open.

By its own account the Russian House in Brussels has been attached to the Russian embassy since the mid-1990s. A Belgian status instrument, a specific centre treaty and any enforcement relating to the house are not documented in public sources.

On 29 March 2022 Belgium expelled 21 Russian embassy and consular staff and implements the EU sanctions. Both measures are documented; neither is attributed to the house. Events were still taking place there in April 2026.

Key finding: Expulsion and cultural operations are separate levers. Belgium shows operational continuity of the house despite far-reaching personnel measures against diplomats.

Estonia No official house established Partner structures kept distinct No Rossotrudnichestvo centre evidenced – but three structures easily confused.

No official Rossotrudnichestvo centre could be verified for Estonia. The Tallinn diplomatic list of June 2026 names the embassy but no science and culture centre.

Three structures must be kept apart: the municipal Vene Kultuurikeskus of the City of Tallinn, a Russkiy Mir centre at a private language school, and the embassy. Their consolidation in 2025 was a municipal organisational decision.

Key finding: Renaming or merging a municipal institution is no substitute for the closure of a Russian House. Establish the legal entity first, then the claim about status.

France Official site active State cooperation suspended Paris suspended cooperation but did not terminate the 1992 treaty.

The 1992 intergovernmental agreement reciprocally establishes a French centre in Moscow and a Russian one in Paris and attaches the latter to the embassy. In 2022 France suspended state cooperation in culture, higher education and science.

According to the official sources examined the agreement was not terminated and the Maison russe not closed; it was open in July 2026. A freezing of accounts in late 2024 was asserted only in Russian reporting and is not officially confirmed.

Key finding: France separates four levels cleanly: programme cooperation, financial sanctions enforcement, diplomatic personnel status and treaty termination. Suspended is not terminated.

Greece Official site active Cultural cooperation suspended The culture minister made it explicit: suspension, not annulment.

The agreement signed in 2001 and ratified in 2005 gives both centres legal capacity and places them under host-state law. International entry into force remained open because the last notification required under Article 16 was not found.

From February 2022 the culture ministry suspended cooperation with Russian cultural organisations. According to reports of her answer in parliament the minister stressed that suspension was not annulment. State language examinations were held at the house in July 2026.

Key finding: A documented intermediate step: state cooperation suspended, without closure and without any traceable termination. Ratification and international entry into force are two different things.

Ireland No official site established Partner and diaspora network active Negotiations in 2010 did not demonstrably produce a centre.

No official Rossotrudnichestvo office or formal Russkiy Mir centre could be evidenced for Ireland. What is documented is that Trinity College announced advanced negotiations on Ireland’s first Russian cultural centre in 2010 – which is not a conclusion.

Visible instead are private cultural organisations and a compatriots network close to the embassy. The “Matryoshka” centre appearing in foundation reports in 2026 is evidence of activity but is not listed as a representation in the formal catalogue.

Key finding: Ireland is a network rather than a location case. Evidence of activity does not establish representative status – and the absence of a property does not make a network politically irrelevant.

Italy Official site active Treaty status open The house in Rome has operated since 2011 – its treaty status is unresolved.

The Russian House in Rome is an official Rossotrudnichestvo institution and has operated in a historic palazzo since November 2011. The classification of the building as private property rests on the operator’s own account and was not independently verified.

Two questions of entry into force remain open in law: for the general 1998 agreement the last notification is missing, and for the specific centre agreement any Italian evidence of ratification or entry into force.

Key finding: Actual operation does not prove the legal existence of a treaty. Ownership of the building and the economic right of use must also be assessed separately.

Latvia Operations ended Property transferred by statute Moscow House was a city representation – not a Rossotrudnichestvo centre.

According to the State Security Service, Moscow House in Riga was the representation of a state-municipal Moscow company whose sole shareholder was the Moscow property authority – not a Rossotrudnichestvo mission.

Since 2022 it has passed through four stages that must be kept apart: frozen economic resource, subject of criminal proceedings, transferred unencumbered to the state by special law, and put up for sale. By the end of September 2025 no buyer had been found.

Key finding: The furthest-reaching enforcement case in this collection – and the most easily mislabelled. A conviction in the requested criminal proceedings is not evidenced.

Lithuania Never regularly opened Demolition proceedings open Never opened – and still not demolished on 1 August 2026.

Moscow House in Vilnius never entered regular operation. It must therefore be described neither as a Russian House closed after February 2022 nor as a former Rossotrudnichestvo presence. The building permit became definitively invalid in 2017.

The demolition is legally ordered but not carried out: in July 2026 a court halted the planned transfer to the city; on 1 August 2026 signatures and judicial confirmation of a privately financed arrangement were missing.

Key finding: Planning and building law can stop a project permanently. But a legally binding demolition order is not a completed demolition – and “never opened” is not a closure.

Luxembourg Official site active Status agreement not public The 2010 status agreement is documented – its wording is not findable.

Two legal acts underpin the centre: the 1993 general agreement approved in 1996, with an express termination clause, and a special agreement of 16 March 2010 on status and operation which, according to the foreign ministry’s annual report, entered into force immediately.

Luxembourg enforces sanctions robustly and expelled a diplomat in 2022; neither the expulsion nor the published freezing figures are attributed to the house. Several events took place there in April 2026.

Key finding: The existence of a status treaty does not confer knowledge of its content. Legal personality, tax rules, notice period and staff privileges remain unknown.

Malta Official site active Temporarily closed in 2022 The 2022 closure was for protection – not by state order.

The centre in Valletta has existed since 1990. The legal trail reaches back to an exchange of notes of 1987 but is largely documented through Russian government decisions approving drafts; signed final texts and their entry into force were not found.

From 3 March 2022 the centre closed temporarily on its own initiative after threats and police intervention. There is no evidence of a state closure order. Events from 2024 to 2026 document resumed on-site operations.

Key finding: A security-driven self-closure is not a state suspension. The exact date of reopening was not found.

Austria Active part of the embassy State cooperation suspended Article 15 of the cultural agreement expressly names the Vienna centre.

Article 15 of the 1998 cultural agreement expressly names the Vienna centre as a cultural centre attached to the Russian embassy. In 2023 the foreign ministry classified it as a notified part of the embassy and applied the Vienna Convention.

Since February 2022 all bilateral state cultural activities have been suspended – a suspension of cooperation, not a closure. The institute was holding events and teaching into the summer of 2026.

Key finding: Legal form and notification weigh more than the “Russian House” label: closing part of a mission would be assessed quite differently in international law than dissolving an association.

Portugal Embassy representation evidenced No separate house established A representative inside the embassy rather than a separate centre.

For Portugal the official Rossotrudnichestvo page names no operation identified as a separate legal person, but a representative on the staff of the Russian embassy – while listing a Lisbon contact address of its own and using the “Russian House” brand.

On 5 April 2022 Portugal expelled ten mission staff. The representative being among them is conceivable but, without a list of names, speculation. Whether regular public operations take place at the contact office remained open.

Key finding: The most important contrast in organisational form: not every structure called “Casa da Rússia” is the official representation – without register evidence the two remain distinct.

Sweden No official house established Former representation disappeared A control case: “not found” is something other than “does not exist”.

The Stockholm diplomatic list of February 2026 names the embassy, the consular section and the trade mission, but no Russian cultural centre. Because it expressly includes cultural institutes of other states, this strengthens the negative finding – without proving non-existence.

All that is documented is a historic “cabinet for Russian language and literature” in Gothenburg, opened with a district administration and the consulate general. Its year of opening could not be verified, nor could any present operation.

Key finding: A “cabinet” of a listed foundation is not a diplomatic cultural centre. Sweden is the test for cleanly stated negative findings.

Slovakia Official site active Legal form and treaty position open The 2022 cooperation halt was lifted again in January 2024.

Bratislava has an official Rossotrudnichestvo representation. The state transparency portal lists it with name and address but states no legal form; no signed centre agreement was found – only a Russian draft of 1996.

In March 2022 the culture ministry prohibited its own organisations from cooperating and lifted that prohibition in January 2024. On-site events in 2025 and 2026 evidence continuing operations.

Key finding: A reversible factual restriction without legal closure. The addition “full operations since June 2025” remains Rossotrudnichestvo’s self-presentation.

Slovenia Physically closed End of treaty open Terminated and closed – the immediate legal effect remains open.

On 22 March 2022 Slovenia initiated termination of the 2011 agreement; according to the foreign ministry the note was delivered on 1 April. The centre in Ljubljana closed on 8 April 2022, a week before the deadline.

The note itself, any Russian reply and an official announcement of the date of termination were not found. On a purely ordinary application of Article 17 the next period would end on 19 September 2026.

Key finding: The only case combining a declaration of termination with actual closure. Physical closure, declaration of termination and the end of the treaty in international law nevertheless remain three different dates – and a partner network continues in Maribor.

Spain Official site active Extent of on-site operations open The most detailed treaty – premises expressly not inviolable.

The 2001 agreement reciprocally governs the Instituto Cervantes in Moscow and the centre in Madrid. It confers the rights of legal persons, places the centres under host-state law and expressly excludes diplomatic inviolability of the premises.

In April 2022 Spain expelled some 25 Russian diplomats and embassy staff; no attribution to the house was found. The house remained active, though no dated on-site event could be evidenced for 2026.

Key finding: The strongest legal comparator: legal capacity is not inviolability, privileges depend on accreditation, and sanctions enforcement is no substitute for treaty termination.

Hungary Official site active Ownership and treaty status open Budapest continues to operate; no measure relating to the house was found.

The Budapest house is an institution embedded in the Russian embassy structure, not a mere partner organisation. The 1993 agreement recognises the centres but refers their support to a separate agreement whose text was not found.

No Hungarian closure, no suspension of the cultural agreement and no asset enforcement specific to the centre were found. A heritage database describes the building as Russian state property – without a land register extract that remains a secondary statement.

Key finding: That no measure was found does not show that all transactions of the house remained permissible or unrestricted.

Cyprus Active official representation Continued treaty validity open Continued presence despite the EU listing; no status agreement findable.

The centre in Nicosia has existed since 1978 and is an official Rossotrudnichestvo representation. After the fire of 2023 – for which the police found no evidence of arson – it is documented holding events in 2024 and again in 2026.

A protocol of 2000 describes the 1972 cultural agreement as continuing in force; the notification date required for its entry into force was not found. A separate status agreement for the centre could not be verified.

Key finding: EU listing, national enforcement and closure are three different levels. The Cypriot sanctions framework of 2025 is general – an individual act against the house is absent.

Russia’s own picture of its European network

Rossotrudnichestvo publishes a map of its Russian Houses and overseas representations on its own website. It shows how the agency presents its presence in Europe — and provides the starting point for comparing that presentation with the structures that can actually be documented.

Russia’s official map of Russian Houses — fact-checked

The red dots are meant to identify cities where Rossotrudnichestvo says it operates a Russian House or overseas representation. The map is not, however, a complete or consistently reliable directory.

Comparing it with the agency’s own country pages already reveals discrepancies. Warsaw, for example, has no red dot, even though Rossotrudnichestvo continues to maintain a dedicated website for the Russian House there, complete with an address, contact details and programme areas.

A missing dot therefore does not prove that no Russian structure exists in a country. A centre may have been closed; an earlier representation may have disappeared; or Russia may continue its work through an embassy, schools, associations, partner organisations or a Russian House in a neighbouring country. Conversely, a dot tells us little about the site’s legal form, size, diplomatic status or current level of activity.

That is why the country files go beyond the current location list. They distinguish between official Russian Houses, Rossotrudnichestvo representations, embassy-based structures, Russkiy Mir centres, “compatriot” networks and local partner organisations.

The legal baseline since 2022

The European Union added Rossotrudnichestvo to its sanctions list on 21 July 2022. That did not amount to an automatic EU-wide closure order. Whether a centre could keep operating also depended on property rights, diplomatic status, bilateral agreements and the measures taken by each host state.

The status labels on this page therefore describe what can be documented on the ground — not the label Russia gives an organisation.

Interim findings after 26 EU country files and one European comparison case

The comparison now shows something else as well: a terminable agreement need not become permanent

The 26 EU Member States examined used very different tools: suspending operations, blocking financial flows, sanctioning property and operators, reducing diplomatic structures, halting a never-opened project under building law, transferring a property by special statute, or allowing a weakly rooted substitute arrangement to fade. Just as often, cooperation was suspended while the house carried on. The Republic of Moldova adds the first European non-EU case in which the treaty-based termination process was carried through in full: the agreement expired and the official Russian House closed.

Moldova also shows the limits of closure. Some education and influence functions can migrate into an embassy or partner structures. The decisive comparison therefore remains broader than “Who closed?” It is: Which legal and political options existed, which were actually used, and which substitute channels remained open?
