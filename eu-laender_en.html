A European comparison

Russian Houses across Europe: How other countries have responded

Germany continues to allow the Russian House in Berlin to operate. Elsewhere in Europe, governments have closed Russian cultural centres, suspended their activities or curtailed official representations. Yet the picture is more complicated than Rossotrudnichestvo’s official presentation suggests. Some countries never had a public-facing Russian House; in others, earlier structures quietly disappeared, or Russia continued its outreach through embassies, associations, schools and hubs in neighbouring countries. These country files examine which tools other governments have used — and what those choices imply for Germany’s handling of the Russian House in Berlin.

Romania Operations ended The Bucharest centre was required to cease operations in 2023.

Bucharest hosted an official Russian Centre for Science and Culture under a bilateral agreement signed in 2013. On 21 February 2023, Romania’s Foreign Ministry summoned the Russian ambassador and informed him that the centre’s activities were being suspended. Operations had to end by 20 August 2023.

The government justified the decision not only by reference to Russia’s war of aggression, but specifically cited repeated disinformation, distorted presentations of historical facts and attempts to justify Russian war crimes. The remaining legal question is how the agreement itself was formally wound down.

Key finding: Romania did not treat the bilateral agreement as an excuse for inaction. It opened a formal procedure and set a clear end date for the centre’s operations.

Czechia Official site active Government scrutiny The Prague centre remains open; no closure has been ordered.

The Russian House in Prague remains an official Rossotrudnichestvo site. It was neither closed nor dissolved after 2022, and it was still advertising events in 2025. Czechia therefore does not belong among the countries that ended their centre’s operations.

At the same time, the house came under official scrutiny over propaganda material and possible sanctions implications. Czech authorities considered adding it to the national sanctions list. No final ban or closure order has been documented.

Key finding: Prague illustrates the difference between subjecting a centre to security scrutiny and actually shutting it down.

Poland Official site active The earlier closure claim was wrong: the Warsaw centre remains active.

Contrary to an earlier version of this press kit, Poland did not close the Russian Centre for Science and Culture in Warsaw. Rossotrudnichestvo continues to list the site, and its management was still appearing at events in Poland in 2025.

Since 2022, Poland has taken forceful action against other Russian diplomatic and state structures. Yet no reliable government decision to close the Warsaw cultural centre itself has been found. The Polish case is a reminder that a hard line on Russia in general does not automatically determine the legal status of a particular Russian House.

Key finding: Even a government with an uncompromising Russia policy may leave its Russian House in operation.

Finland Official site active Financial restrictions Sanctions affected its accounts and property, but the centre remained in place.

The Russian House in Helsinki, opened in 1977, remained an official site after 2022. Its bank accounts were frozen as part of sanctions enforcement. In April 2023, Finnish authorities temporarily seized the land and building, but lifted the measure after a legal review.

Arrangements were later made for property-tax payments, and public activity was again documented in 2025. Finland therefore did not close the house, but it did apply EU sanctions directly to its financial and property-related structures.

Key finding: Helsinki shows the difference between sanctions enforcement and closure: a centre can be hit hard financially and still continue to exist.

Croatia No official house established Former partner structure disappeared The name “Russian House Zagreb” carried more weight than its legal basis.

For a time, a Zagreb-based structure used the name “Russian House” and organised cultural events with local partners. However, no regular Rossotrudnichestvo centre backed by a valid government agreement can be documented. The name therefore projected more institutional weight than the legal structure appears to have possessed.

After Russia’s full-scale invasion in 2022, that public-facing structure largely disappeared. No major closure procedure appears to have been necessary: once political and institutional support fell away, the loosely rooted partner structure largely vanished from public view.

Key finding: Where there is no robust agreement and no protected official house, a Russian cultural brand can quietly vanish without a formal closure process.

Netherlands Former representation disappeared Partner network active No current house, but a decentralised network spanning the embassy, associations and Brussels.

There is no public Russian House in the Netherlands today. An official Rossotrudnichestvo representation can, however, be documented in 2013 and 2014. It appears to have operated without a dedicated cultural building, relying instead on the Russian embassy, the former trade mission, honorary consulates and local partners.

By 2017 at the latest, the country representation had disappeared. Its functions were then spread across a decentralised network: the embassy, the KCOPC/KSORS compatriot council, Russian-language schools and cultural associations, and the Russian House in Brussels. In 2023, the Netherlands also closed the Russian trade mission, one of the venues through which Rossotrudnichestvo had previously operated.

Key finding: The absence of a sign on the door does not mean the absence of a structure. The Dutch model is smaller, more decentralised and harder to see.

Russia’s own picture of its European network

Rossotrudnichestvo publishes a map of its Russian Houses and overseas representations on its own website. It shows how the agency presents its presence in Europe — and provides the starting point for comparing that presentation with the structures that can actually be documented.

Russia’s official map of Russian Houses — fact-checked

The red dots are meant to identify cities where Rossotrudnichestvo says it operates a Russian House or overseas representation. The map is not, however, a complete or consistently reliable directory.

Comparing it with the agency’s own country pages already reveals discrepancies. Warsaw, for example, has no red dot, even though Rossotrudnichestvo continues to maintain a dedicated website for the Russian House there, complete with an address, contact details and programme areas.

A missing dot therefore does not prove that no Russian structure exists in a country. A centre may have been closed; an earlier representation may have disappeared; or Russia may continue its work through an embassy, schools, associations, partner organisations or a Russian House in a neighbouring country. Conversely, a dot tells us little about the site’s legal form, size, diplomatic status or current level of activity.

That is why the country files go beyond the current location list. They distinguish between official Russian Houses, Rossotrudnichestvo representations, embassy-based structures, Russkiy Mir centres, “compatriot” networks and local partner organisations.

The legal baseline since 2022

The European Union added Rossotrudnichestvo to its sanctions list on 21 July 2022. That did not amount to an automatic EU-wide closure order. Whether a centre could keep operating also depended on property rights, diplomatic status, bilateral agreements and the measures taken by each host state.

The status labels on this page therefore describe what can be documented on the ground — not the label Russia gives an organisation.

Interim findings after six country files

The European comparison does not let Germany off the hook — but it must be made precisely

Other governments have used very different tools: suspending an operating agreement, blocking financial flows, reviewing property, reducing diplomatic staffing, or simply allowing a weakly rooted partner structure to wither. Conversely, several official Russian Houses continue to operate despite EU sanctions. The key question is therefore not merely “Who closed?” but: Which legal and political options existed — and which were actually used?
