Press Kit · Russian House Berlin Leadership change · June 2026 As of 07.08.2026
Background

Iswolski leaves, Nekrasova serves as acting head – the underlying issue remains

Pawel Iswolski has left his post as head of the Russian House. His quiet departure coincides with the end date of his documented diplomatic ID card. Whether the two are connected has not been officially confirmed.

In mid-May, Pawel Iswolski was still giving interviews. The director of the Russian House in Berlin, in office since 2017, was speaking as usual: anyone claiming the House engaged in propaganda was, he said, engaging in propaganda themselves. On Instagram he posted photographs from the courtyard, a mural and several Berlin projects. Nothing publicly suggested that he was about to leave Germany.

A few days later, the same man appeared before Russian-language students in Nizhny Novgorod, his home city, and was introduced as the former director.

There was no announcement, no farewell and no public explanation. His Instagram biography suddenly read “Former Director”; his final post was a video entitled “Nine years in nine minutes. Without words.” The long-serving head of the Russian House was gone – and the reason remains publicly unclear.

A diplomatic ID card and its consequences

Iswolski lived in Germany from 2017 to 2022 on an official passport. In May 2022 he received a diplomatic ID card; according to the documents available, it was valid until May 2026. His tenure ended at precisely that point. It has not been officially confirmed whether his diplomatic status was not renewed or whether this had anything to do with his departure.

That may sound like a formality. It is not.

Diplomatic immunity can create a procedural bar to criminal investigations. When the Berlin Public Prosecutor’s Office investigated possible sanctions violations following a complaint by former Green politician Volker Beck, this issue played a central role. Legal scholars Patrick Heinemann and Felix W. Zimmermann had already pointed out in 2023 that proceedings against persons enjoying diplomatic immunity face such a procedural obstacle.

If that status ends, the procedural obstacle ends with it. Heinemann and Zimmermann also identified a political option: where diplomats are suspected of criminal conduct, the Federal Foreign Office could consider declaring them personae non gratae, requiring them to leave Germany.

Iswolski’s quiet departure may indicate that diplomatic status has, for the first time, become an effective lever in this case. There is currently no evidence establishing that this is what happened.

The successor: serving in an acting capacity

Svetlana Nekrasova, previously Iswolski’s deputy, is now running the Russian House on an acting basis. The institution’s own legal notice currently describes her explicitly as “Acting Head”. This does not establish that she has been permanently appointed director.

Correction note

The Russian exile outlet Echo reported on 31 May 2026, citing Wolna, that Nekrasova had become the new director of the Russian House. An earlier version of this page reproduced that account. It does not match the Russian House’s current description of her role or reporting by WDR and Süddeutsche Zeitung on 6 August 2026, both of which describe an acting leadership arrangement. The page has therefore been corrected.

On a Federal Foreign Office diplomatic list reviewed for this research, Nekrasova was recorded with a notification date of 24 October 2020 (version reviewed: May 2022). Her current diplomatic status has not been publicly confirmed.

There is no public evidence that Nekrasova has performed an intelligence role. Her documented career is that of a cultural scholar and cultural official. In the early 2000s she headed the museum department of the Russian Federation’s Ministry of Culture. She later moved to Rossotrudnichestvo and worked for the agency in Vienna and Berlin, among other postings.

Parts of her career were supported by German funding. Nekrasova was a German Chancellor Fellow of the Alexander von Humboldt Foundation in 2002. She also worked as a research associate on the German-Russian project “Russian Museums in the Second World War”, which received funding including from the Volkswagen Foundation. In 2006 she published an article in the journal Osteuropa on the restitution of cultural property between Russia and Ukraine.

That is, first of all, an academic and cultural-policy biography. Today, however, Nekrasova serves as acting head of Rossotrudnichestvo’s Berlin institution – the Russian federal agency under the Ministry of Foreign Affairs that has been on the EU sanctions list since July 2022.

An open position and an applicant

The fact that the leadership has not been permanently filled acquired additional significance in July 2026. WDR and Süddeutsche Zeitung have obtained the French expulsion order against former RT France head Xenia Fedorova. According to their matching reports, the order states that Fedorova had “recently sought the post of director of the Russian House of Science and Culture in Berlin”.

The Russian House said in response to questions that it had no information that Fedorova was to become director. WDR and Süddeutsche Zeitung also reported that Iswolski’s former deputy Nekrasova had meanwhile taken over the leadership on an acting basis.

New French assessment

What the expulsion order also says about the Russian House: France: “Cover for intelligence services”

The building remains, the personnel change

Whether Iswolski runs the House or Nekrasova serves as acting head does not alter the structural core of the issue. The institution on Friedrichstraße continues to be operated by Rossotrudnichestvo. The EU sanctioned the agency in July 2022 and described it in the listing reasons as an important instrument for disseminating Kremlin narratives and propaganda.

The central political question therefore concerns not only the person at the top but also the legal framework governing the House. It consists of five agreements. The 2011 operational agreement can be terminated by ordinary notice; for termination on 6 June 2027, Germany must provide written notice no later than 6 December 2026.

Legal precision

The 2013 property agreement, with its intended 99-year term, contains no unilateral right of ordinary termination. That is not the same as saying it “cannot be terminated under any circumstances”: questions of extraordinary termination or suspension must be considered separately.
