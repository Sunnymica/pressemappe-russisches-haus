Background

Iswolski out, Nekrasova in – the core issue remains

The director of the Russian House has vanished overnight. His quiet exit may mark the first time the German Federal Foreign Office has exercised a mechanism it has held for years.

In mid-May, Pavel Iswolski was still giving interviews. As director of the Russian House in Berlin—a position he held since 2017—he spoke as he always did: asserting that any accusations of propaganda were, in fact, propaganda themselves. On Instagram, he posted photos from the courtyard, a mural, and various Berlin-based projects. Everything seemed business as usual. Publicly, there was no indication that he intended to leave the country.

A few days later, the same man appeared in Nizhny Novgorod, his hometown, addressing Russian language students—introduced as the former director.

No announcement, no farewell, no explanation. His Instagram biography suddenly displayed "Former Director," and his final post was a video titled "Nine years in nine minutes. Without words." The head of what is widely reported to be the largest Russian cultural center outside of Russia has disappeared overnight, and virtually no one knows why.

A Diplomatic Card and its Impact

From 2017 to 2022, Iswolski lived in Germany using an official service passport. In May 2022, he received a diplomatic card; according to available documents, it was valid until May 2026. His tenure has ended exactly now. It has not been officially confirmed whether his diplomatic status was not renewed or if his departure is linked to this fact.

This might sound like a mere formality. It is anything but.

For years, this exact diplomatic status shielded the leadership of the Russian House from prosecution. When the Berlin Public Prosecutor’s Office investigated potential sanctions violations in 2023, following a criminal complaint by former Green Party politician Volker Beck, the case was dismissed. The justification: the officials enjoyed diplomatic immunity. They were effectively out of reach.

If that status is revoked, the procedural obstacle vanishes. Legal experts Patrick Heinemann and Felix W. Zimmermann had already identified this possibility in the Legal Tribune Online in early 2023: in cases where diplomats are involved in criminal activities, the Federal Foreign Office could consider declaring them persona non grata—forcing them to leave Germany. At the time, this was merely a theoretical option.

Iswolski’s quiet departure may be the first time the Federal Foreign Office has shown decisive action in this matter, provided that his diplomatic status was indeed not renewed.

The Successor: A German Educational Background

A successor is already in place. Svetlana Nekrasova, previously Iswolski’s deputy, has taken over the leadership. On the diplomatic list of the Federal Foreign Office, she was recorded with a registration date of October 24, 2020 (as of the version viewed in May 2022); her current status is not publicly confirmed. Her profile differs fundamentally from that of her predecessor—and that is exactly what makes the case so sensitive.

There is no public evidence suggesting that Nekrasova holds an intelligence role. Her documented career is that of a classic cultural scholar. In the early 2000s, she led the museum department at the Ministry of Culture of the Russian Federation. In 2012, she moved to Rossotrudnichestvo, where she took charge of the department for museum and library programs. From 2018, she worked for the Russian federal agency in Vienna, and starting in 2021/22, in Berlin—initially in a senior deputy role, and since 2023, explicitly as Deputy Director for Culture. In this capacity, she organized exhibitions and film series.

What is noteworthy is who largely funded her career path: Germany.

Nekrasova was a German Chancellor Fellow of the Alexander von Humboldt Foundation, an exclusive program for emerging leaders funded by the Federal Foreign Office. Additionally, she worked as a research associate on the project "Russian Museums in World War II," supported by the Volkswagen Foundation. Twenty years ago, she published an article in German in the academic journal Osteuropa regarding the restitution of cultural property—with a closing remark that seems almost surreal today: that Russia and Ukraine should work closely together on this issue. Furthermore, she was a member of the German-Russian Museum Dialogue.

In other words, Germany systematically integrated a Russian cultural official into its academic and cultural policy networks for years. Today, she leads the local branch of an agency that has been on the EU sanctions list since July 2022.

The House Remains, the Personnel Changes

Whether Iswolski leads the house or Nekrasova does not change the core issue. The director may have changed, but the institution itself has not. It remains located on Friedrichstraße and continues to be the largest foreign affiliate of Rossotrudnichestvo, the federal agency of the Russian Ministry of Foreign Affairs. The EU sanctioned this agency in July 2022, labeling it an important channel for state-sponsored Kremlin propaganda.

The decisive question, therefore, does not concern the individual at the helm, but rather the international agreement that enables the existence of this house in the first place. And that agreement can be terminated. The deadline for such action is December 6, 2026.
