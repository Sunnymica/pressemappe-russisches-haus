The Goethe-Institut: No Longer a Shield

The German Federal Government has repeatedly defended its stance on the Russian House by arguing that closing it would trigger immediate retaliation against the Goethe-Instituts in Russia. Does this rationale still hold weight?

The central political argument: “We cannot shut down the Russian House—Russia would retaliate by closing our Goethe-Instituts.” But is this calculation still grounded in reality?
📍 What remains of the Goethe-Instituts in Russia +
  • Novosibirsk: The branch has been completely closed. Premises were abandoned, and only a minimal contact point remains.Forschung & Lehre, May 2023
  • Moscow: All in-person language courses have been suspended. Operations are limited to online offerings, library access, and examinations.Goethe-Institut, 2023
  • St. Petersburg: While in-person courses are technically available, of the three target demographics (adults, teenagers, and children), only one stream remains active. Children’s classes have been moved to the library rather than dedicated classrooms.Independent research, March 2026
  • The Russian Ministry of Foreign Affairs imposed a strict personnel cap on German cultural organizations, forcing the Goethe-Institut to slash its local staff from roughly 200 to a mere 13 employees.Moskauer Deutsche Zeitung, June 2023
  • The Goethe-Institut headquarters officially characterized these measures as the “destruction of the final remaining bridges of civil society” between the two countries.Goethe-Institut press release, May 2023
⚖️ The Asymmetry: Russia’s Presence vs. Germany’s Presence +
  • The Russian House Berlin: Boasting 29,000 m² of prime real estate, it operates a restaurant, cinema, concert hall, language school, and bookstore—running at full capacity as if EU sanctions did not exist.
  • The Goethe-Instituts in Russia: Local staff is capped at 13, the Novosibirsk branch is closed, Moscow offers no classroom teaching, and St. Petersburg is operating on a purely skeletal level.
  • The Russian House continues to enjoy bilateral treaty protection and indirect German taxpayer funding, whereas the Goethe-Instituts are subjected to constant, systemic pressure by Russian authorities.
“They spit on our treaties, yet we cling to the illusion of cultural exchange.”
Robin Wagener, MP (The Greens) — Süddeutsche Zeitung, December 2025
🎯 The Goethe-Institut as a Geopolitical Lever +
  • The Russian Ministry of Foreign Affairs has explicitly threatened to shut down the Goethe-Institut if Germany does not lift the asset freeze imposed on the bank accounts of the Russian House.Federal Foreign Office, Government Press Conference, March 2023
  • Moscow deliberately exploits this threat, and Berlin’s foreign policy actively factors it in—a dynamic that effectively grants Russia the precise leverage it seeks to maintain.
  • Consequently, shielding the Russian House in Berlin does not protect the Goethe-Institut; it merely validates Russia’s coercive tactics.
“It is unacceptable that this propaganda outlet continues to receive funding from our budget while the Russian regime uses it to wage an information war against us.”
Robin Wagener, MP (The Greens) — Süddeutsche Zeitung, December 2025
❓ The Core Question +
  • What civil society “bridges” are realistically being maintained with a regime that actively destabilizes European security, imprisons dissidents, and wages a war of aggression?
  • Who is still able to access the remaining Goethe-Institut services in Russia today, and under what level of state surveillance?
  • Is a cultural presence reduced to a mere fraction of its former self—only 13 staff members nationwide—truly a sound basis for a foreign policy decision that shields a Russian state propaganda hub in the heart of Berlin?
Conclusion The federal government’s “bargaining chip” argument has been hollowed out by reality. The Goethe-Instituts in Russia have already been systematically dismantled—not by German choices, but by Kremlin coercion. Meanwhile, the Russian House in Berlin continues to operate at full capacity. The question is no longer whether we are risking valuable cultural assets. The question is why Germany continues to tolerate this glaring asymmetry.
