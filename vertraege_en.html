The Legal Argument: Fundamental Change of Circumstances

```
⚖️ What Is a “Fundamental Change of Circumstances”? +

Bilateral treaties between nations rest on shared assumptions about mutual relations, common objectives, and a stable political reality. If this foundation changes unpredictably and fundamentally, a treaty may be adapted or terminated altogether, even in the absence of an explicit termination clause.

In international law, this principle is codified in Article 62 of the Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties (VCLT). A state is not bound to a treaty for all eternity under all circumstances, but rather under the conditions that prevailed at the time of its conclusion. This rule applies if the original circumstances constituted an essential basis of the consent of the parties and if the effect of the change is to radically transform the extent of obligations still to be performed.

📋 Why This Applies to the 2013 Agreement +

The 2013 property agreement was drafted during an era when Germany and Russia operated on the assumption of long-term partnership. With Russia’s full-scale invasion of Ukraine on February 24, 2022, this foundational cooperative spirit was permanently shattered.

Russia is waging a war of aggression that violates international law. Rossotrudnichestvo, the operating agency of the Russian House, has been on the EU sanctions list since July 2022. Under these circumstances, Germany would never have agreed to grant a rent-free, long-term right of use to prime real estate in central Berlin. Even Russia could not have deemed this outcome possible in 1992, as evidenced by its commitment to Ukrainian sovereignty under the 1994 Budapest Memorandum.

Attorney Jens Baganz concludes that these developments satisfy all legal criteria for invoking Article 62 of the Vienna Convention.

💡 Termination or Suspension? +

Article 62 permits both avenues. A formal termination would end the treaty completely, thereby revoking the right of use for the property. A suspension would temporarily deactivate the agreement while the conflict persists, offering the political advantage of preserving the underlying legal framework for a potential future normalization of diplomatic ties.

Baganz regards both paths as legally viable. To date, the German federal government has neither evaluated nor refuted this legal recourse. Its frequent reference to the 99-year term limits itself to the text of the agreement while ignoring the principles of customary international law codified in Article 62 VCLT.
